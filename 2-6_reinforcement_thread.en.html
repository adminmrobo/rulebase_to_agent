02

The thread of reinforcement learning

The core of reinforcement learning is very short. Try something, score the result, and adjust the next action toward whatever raises the score. This loop, first seen in the maze robot of Part 1, Ch. 4, has reappeared in almost every chapter since, in changing forms.

quantity ← quantity + (learning rate) × (score received − expected score)
One line that runs through the whole series. Only what gets corrected and who gives the score change from chapter to chapter.

The Q-learning formula of Part 1, Ch. 4 had exactly this shape. Q(s,a) ← Q(s,a) + α[r + γ max Q(s′,·) − Q(s,a)]. GRPO in Ch. 9 was revised to “make answers that score higher than the average among several answers to the same problem more likely,” and that “how much better than the average” is the difference inside the brackets in the formula above. The table below shows where this thread has passed.

WhereWhat gets correctedWho gives the scoreWhen

Reading the table from top to bottom reveals two flows. First, what gets corrected changed from numeric tables → neural network weights → notes and manuals written in text. Second, when it gets corrected moved from the training lab to the work site. That the title of the Reflexion paper in Part 2, Ch. 3 is “Verbal Reinforcement Learning” is no coincidence. Revising verbal notes without touching weights is, structurally, the same loop.

To be precise. The in-operation improvements of Part 2, Chs. 3 and 5 are not reinforcement learning algorithms in the strict sense. The model weights stay the same, and rewards are not mathematically maximized. When this chapter calls it “the same thread,” it means that the structure of try → evaluate → revise is the same. That is why the lessons of reinforcement learning, especially reward hacking in Part 1, Ch. 4 and the overoptimization warning in Ch. 8, apply directly to the operational site as well.
Sources: Sutton & Barto (2018) · Ouyang et al. (2022) · DeepSeek-AI (2025) · Shinn et al. (2023)
03

The journey of rules: follow it yourself

In Part 1, Ch. 1 we drew a loan review flowchart with IF-THEN diamonds. Later, as learning took over the role of rules, the diamonds seemed to disappear. But if you follow the series to the end, the diamonds never disappeared. They only moved. Press the steps below to see where the diamonds moved.

In sum, the role of the rules changed to judgment → scoring → gatekeeper → verifier → memory gate. There is one common point. After Chapter 1, the rule's direct job of deciding “what to do” steadily shrank, and instead the job of deciding “what was right, and what may be done” grew. It is a division of labor: the learned model does the work, and the rules written by people draw the lines.

Why rules came back. In Part 1, Ch. 1, rules failed because it was impossible to write down every case in advance (rule explosion). But rules that check (“Is the answer correct?”, “Was the limit exceeded?”, “Is this command dangerous?”) can be written briefly and clearly. Many problems are hard to build but easy to verify, so rules moved to the place of verification and came to play an even sturdier role. The reason DeepSeek-R1 chose rule-based grading instead of a neural reward model is that a neural grader can be fooled (reward hacking).
Sources: DeepSeek-AI (2025), DeepSeek-R1 paper Sec. 2.2.2 · Part 1, Ch. 1 (Feigenbaum, McDermott) · Part 2, Chs. 2, 3, 5

“What do we delegate, and where do we stop?”

Part 1 was mainly the story of Layers 1 and 2, and Part 2 of Layer 3. And the two threads cut across the layers. Reinforcement learning started in Layer 2 and came down to Layer 3, and rules started in Layer 1 and spread to Layer 2 (scoring) and Layer 3 (gatekeeper and verifier). This map is useful when adopting AI in industry because the place most companies can touch directly is Layer 3. Layers 1 and 2 are the work of the companies that build models; what is in our hands is which tasks to delegate, which rules to check with, and when to hand over to people.

05

Where did the human's place move to?

Each time the machine takes on one more step of its own work, what people do does not disappear but changes position. Below is a concept diagram lining up that change in a single line. Bar length is not a measurement but a relative expression for explanation.

As the bar gets longer, human work moves from “doing it directly” to “defining, verifying, and taking responsibility.” The expert in Part 1, Ch. 1 wrote the rules directly, while the operator in Part 2, Ch. 5 writes the criteria for what may be kept as a skill. The same IF-THEN, but aimed at different targets. In the harness case of Part 2, Ch. 2, what the OpenAI team did most, even without writing code directly, was in the end designing the environment, rules, and inspection mechanisms.

Final experiment: putting the parts into one file

The final experiment assembles the parts so far into one “loan consulting agent” for AbaBot. The customers and figures are all hypothetical, and for the sake of explanation the agent is set up to get the first attempt wrong with the familiar simple interest calculation. The same five customers are processed twice, and the only thing that changes is the criterion for saving a skill. One time only procedures that pass verification are saved; the other time the procedure the agent first wrote is saved without verification. Each line records its basis of judgment (default probability, rule triggered, procedure used) so it can be audited later.

# Part 2, Ch. 6 — Conclusion: assembling the parts of Parts 1 and 2 into one file
# Scenario: AbaBot's "loan consulting agent". Customers and figures are all hypothetical examples.
import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# ① Part 1, Ch. 2 · Trained model: learn the "default probability" from 400 past loans (hypothetical)
X = np.c_[rng.normal(5000, 1500, 400), rng.uniform(0, 0.8, 400)]          # annual income (in units of ten thousand won), debt ratio
y = (rng.random(400) < 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(-2 - 0.0006 * (X[:, 0] - 5000) + 4 * X[:, 1])))).astype(int)
model = LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000).fit((X - X.mean(0)) / X.std(0), y)
def default_prob(income, debt_ratio):
    z = (np.array([[income, debt_ratio]]) - X.mean(0)) / X.std(0)
    return float(model.predict_proba(z)[0, 1])

# ② Part 1, Ch. 1 · Rule: IF-THEN written by people. This time it is not judgment but a 'gatekeeper'
def gate(amount, prob):
    if amount > 50_000_000: return "Blocked", "Over the limit of 5,000 ten-thousand-won units"
    if prob > 0.40:         return "Human review", "Default probability over 40%"
    return "Passed", "Both rules passed"

# Two calculation procedures the agent knows (one is the wrong method)
PROCS = {
    "Simple interest division": lambda a, rate, m: a * (1 + rate * m / 12) / m,                       # wrong
    "Equal installment":  lambda a, rate, m: a * (rate / 12) * (1 + rate / 12) ** m / ((1 + rate / 12) ** m - 1),
}

# ③ Part 2, Ch. 3 · Verifier: not the agent's words, but re-checked with an independent calculation
def verify(answer, amount, rate, months):
    r = rate / 12
    truth = amount * r * (1 + r) ** months / ((1 + r) ** months - 1)
    return abs(answer - truth) < 1

# Fake agent (for explanation): if a stored skill exists, use that procedure; otherwise use the familiar simple interest on the first try, and a different method next time
def agent_try(skills, attempt):
    if "Monthly repayment" in skills: return skills["Monthly repayment"]
    return "Simple interest division" if attempt == 0 else "Equal installment"

# ④⑤ Part 2, Chs. 1, 3, 5 · Agent loop + skill memory. The saving criterion differs by policy
def run(policy):
    skills, log = {}, []
    for name, income, debt, amount, rate, months in CUSTOMERS:
        p = default_prob(income, debt)
        decision, why = gate(amount, p)
        if decision != "Passed":
            log.append(f"{name}: {decision:5s} │ Basis: default prob. {p:.0%}, {why}"); continue
        for attempt in range(3):
            proc = agent_try(skills, attempt)
            pay = PROCS[proc](amount, rate, months)
            ok = verify(pay, amount, rate, months)
            if policy == "Save after verification" and not ok:
                continue                                         # if wrong, retry (not saved)
            if "Monthly repayment" not in skills:
                skills["Monthly repayment"] = proc                        # record the procedure the agent just used as a skill
            mark = "correct" if ok else "wrong"
            log.append(f"{name}: {pay:>11,.0f} won/mo [{mark}] │ Basis: default prob. {p:.0%}, procedure '{proc}', attempt {attempt + 1}")
            break
        else:
            log.append(f"{name}: to a person │ Basis: 3 failed verifications")
    return log, skills

CUSTOMERS = [("Customer A", 6500, 0.20, 30_000_000, 0.048, 36),
             ("Customer B", 3000, 0.75, 20_000_000, 0.051, 24),
             ("Customer C", 5200, 0.30, 80_000_000, 0.048, 60),
             ("Customer D", 4800, 0.25, 15_000_000, 0.054, 12),
             ("Customer E", 7000, 0.10, 40_000_000, 0.048, 48)]

print("[Final experiment] Same 5 customers, only the skill-saving criterion differs")
for policy in ["Save after verification", "Save without verification"]:
    log, skills = run(policy)
    print(f"\n■ {policy} → Saved skills: {skills}")
    for line in log: print("  " + line)

The essence of the series is all contained in the results of the two batches. The [correct] and [wrong] marks were added by us, the readers, when comparing against the correct answers; an agent that saves without verification does not know that it is wrong.

  • Customer A · The agent first computed incorrectly with simple interest, but the verifier (③) caught it, and only the correct procedure from the second attempt was saved as a skill (④). This is the “verify then save” of Part 2, Ch. 5.
  • Customer B · When the learned model (①) output a 90% default probability, the rule (②) handed the case to a person. The machine helps judge, and the final decision is in the hands of a person.
  • Customer C · The default probability is low at 27%, but the amount exceeded the limit, so the rule blocked it. No matter how good the model says the customer is, the line that must not be crossed is kept by the rules written by people.
  • Customers D·E · Thanks to the stored skill, they got it right on the first attempt. This is a loop that gets better with use without repeating the same mistake.
  • Saving without verification · Once Customer A's first attempt (simple interest) became a skill as-is, Customers D·E also confidently produced wrong amounts using the same method. This is a loop that gets worse with use. Customers B and C are the same in both cases, because the rule gatekeeper keeps the line regardless of the saving criterion.
  • Basis log · The “basis” recorded on each line is the minimal audit record that lets a risk manager later trace back “why this customer was passed to a person” and “what procedure was used to calculate.”
What this experiment does not show. Real loan reviews come with conditions such as credit rating regulations, the duty to explain, and anti-discrimination rules; a default prediction model itself must also pass fairness verification. Here it has been reduced to a very small size only to show how the parts fit together.

08

Remaining questions

The series ends, but the questions remain open. As of 2026, here are four things that researchers and practitioners are grappling with together.

QuestionWhy it is hard
What is the score for work that cannot be verified?Math problems and code can be graded with rules, so reasoning models and loops have developed quickly (Part 1, Ch. 9; Part 2, Ch. 3). But good strategy reports, good consultations, and good writing are hard to grade with short rules. For such work one has to depend again on human preferences or neural graders, and the overoptimization problem of Part 1, Ch. 8 comes back to life.
Reward hacking in operationJust as the boat game AI in the training lab chased only the score and spun in circles (Part 1, Ch. 4), an agent in the field can also chase only the front when “passing the test” and “the work actually being done” diverge. Designing the verifier is the same as designing the reward.
Who manages the accumulated memory?As skills and memory accumulate over months and years, outdated procedures and wrongly hardened habits pile up too (Part 2, Ch. 5). Just as human organizations revise manuals regularly, an agent's memory also needs audit and retirement procedures.
How long can we delegate?METR measured that the length of tasks AI can complete has doubled roughly every seven months (Part 2, Ch. 1). The longer the time delegated, the more important it becomes how to set the interval of intermediate checks and the stopping conditions (Part 2, Ch. 3).

Field adoption checklist

AbaBot's one-line principle, "Models do the work, rules guard the limits, people take responsibility," broken down into the order of real adoption, gives seven steps. Go down from the top one at a time; you move to the next step only when the answer is "yes." Next to each step, the chapter in this series that covers its basis is listed.

#StepQuestion to checkIf "no"Basis
1Choose the task to delegateCan the result be checked against rules (calculations, wording, format checks, for example)?For tasks that are hard to check (strategic judgment, counseling), delegate only the first draft and have a person finish itPart 1, Chapter 9 · Part 2, Chapter 3
