Orchestration:
conductor agent and subagents

An orchestra's conductor doesn't play an instrument directly. They assign parts of the piece, keep the beat, and listen to the overall sound. Through Ch. 3, one agent ran the loop. This chapter looks at the structure of assigning work to multiple agents, and when that helps and when it hurts.

01

Work that's hard to do alone

In Ch. 1 we saw that as steps get longer, the success probability falls as pk. In Ch. 2 we saw that as records pile up, the context window overflows and the middle gets blurry. When one agent holds a big job for a long time, both problems grow at once.

Orchestration solves this by splitting the work. An agent in the conductor role (the orchestrator) breaks a big task into pieces and hands them to several subagents. Each does its share in a clean context and reports back only a short summary. The conductor gathers those summaries to complete the whole.

Machine analogy. After the new hire of Ch. 1, the company of Ch. 2, and the work cycle of Ch. 3, Ch. 4 is about a team. The team lead splits the project and assigns pieces to team members. Each member focuses on their own task, a quality person reviews separately, and the lead gathers the results and writes the report.

The structure of conductor and players

In 12/2024, Anthropic summarized five agent structures in real use. The conductor-worker structure in this chapter is one of them, and the others are used too.

03

Case for: 90.2% improvement

On June 13, 2025, Anthropic published the structure of its research feature. A conductor agent (Claude Opus 4) analyzes the question, sets a strategy, launches several subagents (Claude Sonnet 4) to search at the same time, and gathers the results. A separate agent handles attributing sources.

Performance

+90.2%

In internal research evaluations, the multi-agent structure scored 90.2% better than a single Claude Opus 4 agent. Example: tasks that must be searched in many directions at once, like "find the board members of IT companies in the S&P 500."

Key

Tokens at 80%

In web browsing evaluations, 80% of the performance gap was explained by one factor: "how many tokens were used." The subagents read and thought more, each within its own context.

Cost

About 15x

Compared with ordinary chat, agents use about 4x the tokens and multi-agent systems about 15x. So use them only for work that's worth that.

The same post clearly noted its limits. It said the approach doesn't fit fields where all agents must share the same context or where agents are heavily entangled, and that most coding work has fewer parts that can be split than research does. It also admitted an early mistake of launching 50 subagents for a simple question.Source: Anthropic (2025.6.13), "How we built our multi-agent research system"

04

Case against: don't build multi-agents

The day before, on June 12, 2025, Walden Yan of Cognition, maker of the coding agent Devin, published "Don't Build Multi-Agents." His example goes like this.

Instruction

"Build Flappy Bird"

The conductor splits it in two. Subagent 1: a background with pipes. Subagent 2: a bird moving up and down.

Result

Two pieces that don't fit

Subagent 1 misread the instruction and built a Super Mario-like background, and subagent 2 made a bird that fit neither the game's art style nor its motion. The conductor had to force the two pieces together.

Yan put forward two principles. First, share context: pass the entire record of work so far, not just a single message. Second, actions carry hidden decisions: each agent's action rests on its own assumptions, and when assumptions conflict, results get worse.Source: Yan (2025.6.12), Cognition "Don't Build Multi-Agents"

Assistant (Haru)Two opposite posts came out a day apart. Who's right?
DoctorBoth are. Anthropic's work was independent searches; Cognition's work was code that has to interlock tightly. The question isn't "split or don't split," it's "how tangled is this work." Let's measure it ourselves in the next section.
05

Compare directly

Suppose a weekly report is split into 6 subtasks, and all 6 must be correct for the report to succeed. Done alone, mistakes grow as the record piles up, and self-review catches only 30% of them. Split up, each part gets a clean context, so mistakes are fewer and an independent verifier catches 70%. But the more the tasks are entangled, the more information leaks at each handoff and the more the parts wait on each other.

Success rate = Πi [ 1 − ei(1 − d + dei) ]
ei is the error probability of subtask i, and d is the probability that verification catches an error. A caught error is redone once, and it can fail again. Solo: ei = 0.04 + 0.025i, d = 0.3. Split: e = 0.04 + 0.15c, d = 0.7 (c: degree of entanglement between the tasks).
MethodSuccess rateTime takenTokens
Solo62.2%642,000
Split · entanglement 092.4%3135,000 (3.2x)
Split · entanglement 0.576.5%5147,000 (3.5x)
Split · entanglement 159.7%8159,000 (3.8x)

When entanglement is low, splitting is more accurate and twice as fast. As entanglement grows, both accuracy and speed get worse than solo, and tokens still rise by more than 3x. The reason Anthropic and Cognition reached opposite conclusions a day apart lies at the two ends of this graph.

06

How things break when many agents work together

In 2025, a UC Berkeley team led by Cemri analyzed about 1,600 execution records from seven popular multi-agent frameworks. They classified the failure causes into 14 types and grouped them into three clusters.

Cluster 1

System design problems

Vague role definitions, violating task specs, repeating work already done, not knowing when to stop, and so on. These are cases where the conductor handed out the score wrongly.

Cluster 2

Misalignment between agents

Not sharing needed information, ignoring other agents' opinions, conversation drifting off course, and so on. The background and bird of Flappy Bird are this case.

Cluster 3

Verification failures

Skipping verification entirely, or doing it so sloppily that wrong results pass as "done." This is the false completion from Ch. 3, happening at team scale.

Source: Cemri et al. (2025), "Why Do Multi-Agent LLM Systems Fail?"
Fruit Fly ResearcherAll three clusters line up exactly with the earlier chapters of Part 2. Design problems are Ch. 2 (harness), misalignment is Ch. 2 (context), and verification failures are Ch. 3 (loop). Adding more people doesn't make that homework go away.

Python lab notes

Example 1 is a skeleton, using only the standard library, of a conductor running three subagents at the same time. The subagents are fakes that just wait a set time instead of calling a real LLM, but the time difference from running concurrently is real. Example 2 is the calculation model from Section 5.

orchestrator.pysplit and run at the same time · when it pays off
# Part 2, Ch. 4 — Orchestration: collection of runnable examples
import time
from concurrent.futures import ThreadPoolExecutor

# ── Example 1. Conductor and subagents: split and run at the same time ─────────────────────
SUBTASKS = {  # subagent name: (assigned job, time taken in seconds — kept short for illustration)
    "Assistant (Haru)":     ("collect rate and FX data", 0.6),
    "AbaBot":     ("calculate returns and build table", 0.5),
    "Fruit Fly Researcher":  ("summarize 3 related news items", 0.7),
}

def subagent(name, job, sec):
    time.sleep(sec)                                   # in reality, the time the LLM spends working with tools
    return f"[{name}] {job} done (reports 3-line summary only)"   # passes only a short summary to the conductor

def orchestrator(parallel=True):
    t0 = time.time()
    if parallel:
        with ThreadPoolExecutor() as ex:
            reports = list(ex.map(lambda kv: subagent(kv[0], *kv[1]), SUBTASKS.items()))
    else:
        reports = [subagent(n, j, s) for n, (j, s) in SUBTASKS.items()]
    return reports, time.time() - t0

print("[Example 1] Conductor (Doctor) splits the weekly market report and assigns it out")
for mode in (False, True):
    reports, sec = orchestrator(parallel=mode)
    print(f"  {'parallel' if mode else 'sequential'}: {sec:.2f}s")
for r in reports:
    print("   ", r)

# ── Example 2. When is splitting worth it? (calculation model) ──────────────────────
# Assumptions: 6 subtasks. All must be correct for the report to succeed.
#  · Solo: mistakes grow as the record piles up, e_i = 0.04 + 0.025·i; self-review catches only 30% of mistakes
#  · Split: fresh context each time, so mistakes are 0.04; instead, handoff loss 0.15·c from entanglement c,
#               an independent verifier catches 70% of mistakes and has them redone once
#  · Tokens: each subagent searches and explores on its own, so 20,000 tokens apiece (an assumption set to give a ratio similar to the 'agent about 4x,
#          multi-agent about 15x' that Anthropic reported)
def solo(n=6, catch=0.3):
    p = 1.0
    for i in range(n):
        e = 0.04 + 0.025 * i
        p *= 1 - e * (1 - catch + catch * e)          # missed it, or caught it and redid it but got it wrong again
    return p, n, sum(2000 * (i + 1) for i in range(n))    # success rate, time, tokens

def team(c, n=6, catch=0.7):
    e = 0.04 + 0.15 * c
    p = (1 - e * (1 - catch + catch * e)) ** n
    waves = 1 + round(c * (n - 1))                    # the more entangled, the more they must wait and go in turns
    time_ = 1 + waves + 1                             # plan + execute + gather
    tokens = n * 20000 + 6000 + n * 1500 + n * 4000 * c  # subagents (each explores) + conductor + verification + context sharing
    return p, time_, tokens

print("\n[Example 2] Solo vs. split (degree of entanglement c)")
p, t, k = solo()
print(f"  solo          success {p:5.1%}  time {t:4.1f}  tokens {k:7,.0f}")
for c in [0.0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0]:
    p, t, k = team(c)
    print(f"  split c={c:.2f}  success {p:5.1%}  time {t:4.1f}  tokens {k:7,.0f}  ({k / solo()[2]:.1f}x)")

08

AbaBot's case and its limits

In the field there are cases in the opposite direction. A fintech founder who tried to run a company without staff set up five separate agents for marketing, sales, community support, and so on. But the agents couldn't share context, so marketing's tone and the sales messages were completely different, the same skill was duplicated in each agent, and fixing one broke things elsewhere. He shut all five down and consolidated them into one Hermes agent, and says the brand tone built up in marketing carried over into the sales messages.Source: GPTers, "Hermes Agent Complete Guide: Hermes vs OpenClaw Comparison" (2026.4), introducing a post by Nick Spisak

AbaBotI thought more agents always meant better results, but if five run separately, it's actually a net loss.
DoctorEven an orchestra turns to noise if it doesn't share the score. So these days the direction is "split the work, but gather memory and skills in one place." The leading example of that is Hermes, in the next chapter.
