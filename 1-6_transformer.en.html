01

The forgetfulness of a machine that reads one word at a time

The bigram in Chapter 5 looked only at the one word right before. The improvement on it was the recurrent neural network (RNN) of the 1980s to 2010s. An RNN reads words one at a time and carries a single memory vector that summarizes what it has read so far into the next word.

ht = tanh( W ht−1 + U xt )
New memory = a mix of (previous memory + current word). The whole sentence gets pressed, layer by layer, into one vector.

There were two problems. First, forgetfulness. Like the game of telephone, the earlier content fades a little with each word that passes. During training, the backpropagation signal from Chapter 3 also traveled back dozens of steps and shrank toward zero. In 1997, Hochreiter and Schmidhuber's LSTM added "a gate that decides what to keep and what to erase," which improved things a lot, but the basic structure stayed the same. Second, slowness. You had to read the previous word before reading the next, so the GPU "many calculations at once" from Chapter 3 could not really be used. Source: Hochreiter & Schmidhuber (1997)

Machine analogy. An RNN is a machine that reads a book through a narrow window that shows only one line at a time. It writes what has passed on a single memory note, but the note has limited space, so older content fades first. The Chapter 6 machine spreads out a whole page of the book and turns its eyes to whatever word it needs.
02

Attention: picking out the words you need

In 2014, Bahdanau, Cho, and Bengio added a small device to a translation network. Each time it writes a word of the translation, it scans the whole source sentence again and gives more weight to the source words it needs right now. There was no longer a need to press everything into one memory vector. This is where attention began. Source: Bahdanau, Cho & Bengio (2014)

In 2017, Vaswani and seven others at Google went a step further in the paper "Attention Is All You Need." They removed the RNN entirely and built a translation model using only self-attention, in which every word in the sentence looks at every other word simultaneously. This structure is the Transformer. It set the record at the time on the English-to-German translation benchmark (WMT 2014) with a BLEU score of 28.4, and training the large model took 3.5 days on 8 GPUs. Source: Vaswani et al. (2017)

03

Query, key, value: Q · K · V

Attention is a lot like finding material in a library. For each word, you make three cards.

Query

Query card q

"What information do I need right now?" For bank: "I'm a riverbank or a financial bank? Give me a clue."

Key

Key card k

"Here is the information I hold." For river: "Here are clues about shorelines and nature."

Value

Value card v

The information actually handed over when a key matches the query well. It is taken in a mix proportional to how well they match.

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( QK⊤ / √d ) V
① QK⊤: multiply every query by every key at once to build a "relevance score table" (same idea as cosine similarity in Chapter 5). ② Divide by √d so the scores don't get too large. ③ Use softmax to turn each row into weights that sum to 1. ④ Mix the content V using those weights. Source: Vaswani et al. (2017), Eq. (1)
Q = XWQ,  K = XWK,  V = XWV
The three cards are made by multiplying the word vectors X by three matrices. These matrices are the part that is actually learned. The machine learns from data "what to ask, and what to tag as a key." Just as in Chapter 3 the machine found features on its own, here it finds where to look on its own.

05

The structure of one Transformer layer

A Transformer stacks layers of the same shape many times. The base model in the original paper had 6 layers, 512-dimensional word vectors, and 8 attention heads.

PartWhat it does
Positional encodingAttention doesn't know order ("a dog bites a person" and "a person bites a dog" come out the same). So it adds a wave pattern of sin and cos, different at each position, to the word vectors to convey order.
Multi-headSeveral sets (heads) of attention each look at a different kind of relationship. One head might look at "who did it," another at "what is being pointed to."
FeedforwardThe information gathered by attention is processed once more, word by word, by a three-layer neural network from Chapter 3.
Residual connectionThe newly computed value is added to the original value. In the Section 4 experiment, adding context to the original bank vector is exactly this. It keeps training from collapsing even when the layers are stacked deep.
PE(pos, 2i) = sin(pos / 100002i/d),  PE(pos, 2i+1) = cos(pos / 100002i/d)
Multiple wave cycles from fast to slow are mixed with sin and cos, creating a fingerprint for each position that does not overlap. It's like reading the second, minute, and hour hands of a clock together to know the exact time.
06

Hiding the future: the next-word predictor

In Chapter 5 we said language models "guess the next word." But in self-attention, every word sees every word. If left alone, the answer (the next word) would be peeked at in advance. So each word is allowed to see only the words before it, and the upper-right half of the score table is blanked out. This is called a causal mask.

theriverbankwas the●··· river●●·· bank●●●· was●●●●

This way, one sentence lets us solve four problems at once: "the → river," "the river → bank," "the river bank → was," and so on. An RNN had to solve these four problems one after another. This parallelism is the secret that let the model grow enormously in Chapter 7.

2018 · Google

BERT

Trained by looking at both sides without a mask, guessing a word hidden in the middle of a sentence. Strong at understanding sentences.

2018 · OpenAI

GPT

Trained with a causal mask, looking only at what came before to predict the next word. Strong at generating sentences. It is the direct ancestor of ChatGPT.

Python lab notes

Examples 1, 2, and 4 use only numpy. Example 3 reuses the GloVe vectors from Chapter 5.

Examples 1 and 2. Self-attention in 10 lines and the causal mask

self_attention.pysoftmax(QKᵀ/√d)V
import numpy as np
softmax = lambda s: np.exp(s - s.max(-1, keepdims=True)) / np.exp(s - s.max(-1, keepdims=True)).sum(-1, keepdims=True)

def self_attention(X, Wq, Wk, Wv, mask=None):
    Q, K, V = X @ Wq, X @ Wk, X @ Wv          # question · tag · content
    S = Q @ K.T / np.sqrt(K.shape[1])          # relevance of every word pair
    if mask is not None:
        S = np.where(mask, S, -1e9)             # erase cells that must not be seen
    A = softmax(S)                              # weights that sum to 1 per row
    return A @ V, A

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
X = rng.normal(size=(4, 8))                     # 4 words, 8 dimensions each
Wq, Wk, Wv = (rng.normal(size=(8, 8)) for _ in range(3))
out, A = self_attention(X, Wq, Wk, Wv)
print("[Example 1] Attention weights (row = word looking, column = word seen)")
print(A)
print("  Row sums:", A.sum(1))


causal = np.tril(np.ones((4, 4), dtype=bool))
_, A2 = self_attention(X, Wq, Wk, Wv, mask=causal)
print("\n[Example 2] Attention weights with causal mask (only earlier words visible)")
print(A2)
Output

Example 3. How context changes the meaning of bank

contextual_bank.pyGloVe + attention + residual connection
from gensim.models import KeyedVectors

kv = KeyedVectors.load("data/glove50.kv")
S1 = "he sat on the river bank and watched the water".split()
S2 = "she went to the bank to get a loan and some money".split()
FUNC = {"he", "she", "on", "the", "and", "to", "a", "some", "get", "went", "sat", "watched"}
cos = lambda a, b: float(a @ b / np.linalg.norm(a) / np.linalg.norm(b))

def bank_in_context(sent, focus_content):
    Xs = np.array([kv[w] for w in sent])
    i = sent.index("bank")
    s = Xs @ Xs[i] / np.sqrt(Xs.shape[1])        # score for how much bank looks at other words
    s[i] = -1e9                                   # leave out itself
    if focus_content:                             # hand imitation of what learned W_Q, W_K do:
        s = np.where([w in FUNC for w in sent], -1e9, s)   # do not look at function words
    w = softmax(s)
    return Xs[i] + w @ Xs, w                      # residual connection: original meaning + context

print("\n[Example 3] Does the meaning of bank shift with context? (river similarity − money similarity)")
b = kv["bank"]
print(f"  Fixed vector (Ch. 5)        : {cos(b, kv['river']) - cos(b, kv['money']):+.3f}")
for label, focus in [("Attention before training", False), ("Focus on content words", True)]:
    for name, S in [("riverbank sentence", S1), ("loan sentence", S2)]:
        v, w = bank_in_context(S, focus)
        top = ", ".join(f"{t} {x:.2f}" for t, x in sorted(zip(S, w), key=lambda t: -t[1])[:3])
        print(f"  {label} · {name}: {cos(v, kv['river']) - cos(v, kv['money']):+.3f}   most-seen words: {top}")
for name, S in [("riverbank sentence", S1), ("loan sentence", S2)]:
    v, _ = bank_in_context(S, True)
    near = [w for w, _ in kv.similar_by_vector(v, topn=8) if w not in ("bank", "banks")][:5]
    print(f"  Words close to bank in the {name} (among 400,000): {', '.join(near)}")
Output

Example 4. Positional encoding

positional_encoding.pyA sin·cos fingerprint that differs by position
def positional_encoding(n_pos, d):
    pos = np.arange(n_pos)[:, None]
    i = np.arange(d // 2)[None, :]
    ang = pos / (10000 ** (2 * i / d))
    pe = np.zeros((n_pos, d))
    pe[:, 0::2], pe[:, 1::2] = np.sin(ang), np.cos(ang)
    return pe
PE = positional_encoding(6, 8)
print("\n[Example 4] Positional encoding (positions 0~5, first 4 dimensions)")
print(PE[:, :4])
Output (positions 0~5, first 4 dimensions)
[[ 0.    1.    0.    1.  ]
 [ 0.84  0.54  0.1   1.  ]
 [ 0.91 -0.42  0.2   0.98]
 [ 0.14 -0.99  0.3   0.96]
 [-0.76 -0.65  0.39  0.92]
 [-0.96  0.28  0.48  0.88]]

The first column (fast wave) changes a lot from position to position, and the third column (slow wave) changes slowly. Looking at several speeds together, you can tell which position it is without overlap.

Field cases and limits of AbaBot

AbaBotTransformers are already making money for you every day. In the search box you use!
2017 · Translation

Who is "it"?

In "The animal didn't cross the street because it was too tired" and "…because it was too wide," "it" refers to the animal and the street, respectively. When translated into French, il or elle is chosen by gender. The Transformer used attention to find the noun that "it" points to and translated both correctly.

Google AI Blog (2017)
2019 · Search

Google Search with BERT

In "2019 brazil traveler to usa need a visa," the key is the small word "to." Older search showed results for Americans going to Brazil, but BERT read the direction and found visa information for Brazilian travelers to the US.

Applied to 1 in 10 US English searches
Finance

Reading filings and news

In sentences where meaning turns on the surrounding context, such as "Despite the rate cut, earnings were weak," a model that uses context vectors has been reported to read sentiment more accurately than one that counts words one by one. BERT-family models further trained on financial documents are used for this kind of task.

e.g., FinBERT (Araci, 2019)
Source: Google AI Blog (2017), "Transformer: A Novel Neural Network Architecture for Language Understanding" · Nayak (2019), Google blog "Understanding searches better than ever before" · Araci (2019)

Limitation: the cost of everyone seeing everyone

If n words all see each other, the score table has n2 cells. 1,000 words means 1 million cells; 100,000 words means 10 billion cells. That is why language models have a limit on how long a sequence they can see at once (context window). This is also why context engineering, deciding what goes inside the window, becomes important in Part 2 with the harness.
