Buying thinking time:
reasoning models

The inference engine in Chapter 1 chained conclusions by checking them against rules written by people. Reasoning models of 2024 write out their solutions at length by themselves, look back at mistakes, and try multiple paths. Yet the judge that trained these models was once again "rules." The two ends of the series title meet in this chapter.

01

Answer right away vs. work it out

Chapter 5 explained that a language model is a machine that predicts the next word, one token at a time. The computation used to produce one token is fixed. So if you have it write "Answer: 75" right away, it has to do both the multiplication and the subtraction within the computation of a token or two. But if you have it write the solution first, "23×4=92, 92−17=75," the intermediate results remain as text, and the next token can compute by looking at them. Writing out the solution itself gives it time to compute and a scratchpad.

In 2022, Kojima's team found that merely adding one sentence, "Let's think step by step," to the end of a question greatly raised the accuracy.

Machine analogy. The machine in Chapter 9 is an exam taker who is given scratch paper. Machines up to Chapter 7 had to write just the answer on the answer sheet as soon as they saw the question. Now they can write solutions on scratch paper, erase mistakes, and try again. Instead of spending more exam time (computation), they become more accurate.
02

Chain of thought

That same year, Google's Wei team applied the in-context learning from Chapter 7. Instead of writing only answers in the examples placed before the question, they wrote out the solution steps too. When the model was shown this, it also began writing solutions before answering. This is called chain-of-thought (CoT).

Examples with answers only

Standard prompt

Q: I had 5 balls and bought 2 more tubes with 3 balls each. How many balls do I have now?
A: 11

Q: From 4 boxes of 23 apples, I ate 17…
A: 82

Examples with solution steps too

Chain of thought

Q: I had 5 balls and bought 2 more tubes with 3 balls each. How many balls do I have now?
A: 2×3=6, 5+6=11. The answer is 11.

Q: From 4 boxes of 23 apples, I ate 17…
A: 23×4=92, 92−17=75. The answer is 75.

Interestingly, this effect is almost absent in small models and clearly appears in large models of about 100 billion parameters or more. The "emergence" debate from Chapter 7 comes up again here. Even when asked to write out steps, the model needs the skill to write each step correctly for it to help.Source: Wei et al. (2022), "Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models"

03

Solve multiple times and take a majority vote

People also solve hard problems two or three times, and feel reassured when the same answer comes out. In 2022, Wang's team proposed self-consistency: have the model solve the same problem several times with chain-of-thought, then choose the most frequent answer. On GSM8K, accuracy rose 17.9 percentage points compared with chain-of-thought alone.Source: Wang et al. (2022)

Final answer = argmaxa  Σi=1n 1[ answeri = a ]
Count the answers from n attempts and choose the most frequent one. It works because correct solutions converge on the same answer, while incorrect solutions scatter across different answers.
04

Thinking time can also be scaled: o1

The methods in Sections 2–3 were "ways of use": changing prompts or running many times. On September 12, 2024, OpenAI released o1, which built this into the model. It was trained with reinforcement learning to write long chains of thought on its own before answering. OpenAI stated two things: performance keeps steadily rising as reinforcement learning is increased (training compute), and as the model is given more thinking time (inference compute).

Performance ↑  ←  training compute ↑  +  inference compute (thinking time) ↑
The scaling law in Chapter 7 was "scale up when training." Reasoning models added a second knob: "take your time when answering." It's as if a sixth ingredient was added to the five ingredients in Section 1 of Chapter 7.
the Assistant (Haru)From 12% to 74%! But how did o1 learn to think that long? Did people compare the solutions one by one, like in Chapter 8?
DoctorOpenAI did not disclose the detailed training method. But four months later, DeepSeek in China built a model at a similar level and published the whole method in a paper. A very interesting name shows up again there.
05

The judge is rules again: DeepSeek-R1

The core of the DeepSeek-R1 paper, published by DeepSeek in January 2025, was surprisingly simple. Without human-written solution examples or the reward model from Chapter 8, they had the base language model solve many math and coding problems and ran reinforcement learning with rule-based grading (R1-Zero).

Accuracy reward

Is the answer right?

For math, the final answer is compared with the correct one; for coding, the code is compiled and tests are run. No human judgment is needed.

IF answer = correct THEN +1
Format reward

Was the thinking written inside the tags?

A format rule that the solution be written inside <think> … </think> and the answer be written after it.

IF format followed THEN +α
What was not used

Neural network reward model

The paper said it did not use a neural network reward model because "neural network reward models may suffer from reward hacking in large-scale reinforcement learning." That is exactly the problem in Section 5 of Chapter 8.

Lesson from Chapter 8
Source: DeepSeek-AI (2025), Section 2.2.2, Reward Modeling

The learning algorithm is GRPO, which refines the reinforcement learning from Chapter 4 for language models. The same problem is solved several times (as a group), and each solution's score is compared with the group average. Solutions better than average are reinforced more often, and worse ones less often. Unlike Chapter 8, no separate neural network that predicts scores is needed.

Ai = ( ri − mean(r1, …, rG) ) / std(r1, …, rG)
Group-relative score (advantage). It is the same idea as a standard score, converting test scores to the class mean and standard deviation. On problems where all answers are right or all are wrong, it becomes 0 and nothing is learned; the most learning happens on problems where the model is sometimes right and sometimes wrong.Source: Shao et al. (2024) · DeepSeek-AI (2025)
DeepSeek-R1-Zero · AIME 2024Accuracy
Start of reinforcement learning (1 attempt)15.6%
After reinforcement learning (1 attempt)71.0%
After reinforcement learning (multiple attempts, majority vote)86.7%

No one taught it to "double-check" or "look back," but as training progressed, the model's solutions grew longer and it began to find and fix its own mistakes mid-solution. The paper quotes the model writing mid-solution, "Wait, wait. That's an aha moment."Source: DeepSeek-AI (2025), Section 2.2.4

Fruit Fly ResearcherWait. "IF answer = correct THEN +1"? That's the IF-THEN rule from Chapter 1.
DoctorExactly! In the first chapter you doubted that rules could have disappeared so easily. You were right. The rules didn't disappear. They moved from the judge's seat to the referee's bench. In the 1970s, rules gave the answer; in 2025, rules grade the answer.

Python lab notes

Uses only the standard library re and numpy.

Example 1. Rule-based grader

rule_reward.pyChapter 1's IF-THEN, this time as the judge
import re

def rule_reward(response, answer):
    r = 0.0
    if re.search(r"<think>.+</think>", response, re.S):        # format rule: thinking inside tags
        r += 0.1
    m = re.search(r"답:\s*(-?\d+)", response)                   # pull out the last '답: number'
    if m and int(m.group(1)) == answer:                          # if it matches the correct answer
        r += 1.0
    return r

Q, ANSWER = "From 4 boxes holding 23 apples each, 17 apples were eaten. How many apples are left?", 75
cands = {
    "answer right away":      "답: 82",
    "answer after solving":   "<think>23×4=92, 92−17=75</think> 답: 75",
    "wrong solution":    "<think>23×4=82, 82−17=65</think> 답: 65",
    "ignores format":    "23 곱하기 4는 92이고 17을 빼면 75. 답: 75",
}
print("[Example 1] Scores from the rule-based grader")
for name, resp in cands.items():
    print(f"  {name:8s} → {rule_reward(resp, ANSWER):.1f}")
Output

wrong solution    → 0.1

Example 2. Multiple attempts and majority vote

self_consistency.pyAssumes wrong answers scatter across 4 options
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

def vote_accuracy(p, n, n_wrong=4, trials=20000):
    ok = 0
    for _ in range(trials):
        right = rng.random(n) < p
        votes = np.zeros(n_wrong + 1)
        votes[0] = right.sum()
        wrong_ans = rng.integers(1, n_wrong + 1, size=(~right).sum())
        np.add.at(votes, wrong_ans, 1)
        best = np.flatnonzero(votes == votes.max())
        ok += rng.choice(best) == 0                     # ties broken at random
    return ok / trials
print("\n[Example 2] Single-attempt accuracy p, majority vote over n attempts")
print("    p     n=1    n=5   n=15   n=64")
for p in [0.3, 0.5, 0.7]:
    print(f"  {p:.1f} " + " ".join(f"{vote_accuracy(p, n, trials=4000):6.1%}" for n in [1, 5, 15, 64]))

Example 3. Building a reasoning habit with rule-based rewards alone (GRPO toy)

grpo_toy.pyFixing habits with group-relative scores
import numpy as np

STRAT = ["Direct answer", "Step by step", "Step by step + check", "Repeating the same words"]
ACC   = np.array([0.30, 0.65, 0.90, 0.45])     # per-strategy accuracy
LENG  = np.array([10,   80,   160,  300])      # per-strategy solution length (tokens)
FMT   = np.array([0,    1,    1,    1])        # whether the <think> format is followed
G, lr = 8, 0.3                                  # solve one problem 8 times and compare within the group

def train(seed, steps=400, record=False):
    rg = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    theta = np.array([2.0, 0.0, -1.5, 0.0])     # at first the 'direct answer' habit is strong and 'check' is rare
    hist = []
    for step in range(steps + 1):
        pi = np.exp(theta) / np.exp(theta).sum()
        if record and step % 25 == 0:
            hist.append([step] + [round(float(x), 3) for x in pi]
                        + [round(float(pi @ LENG), 1), round(float(pi @ ACC), 3)])
        picks = rg.choice(4, size=G, p=pi)                          # solve the same problem 8 times
        rewards = (rg.random(G) < ACC[picks]) * 1.0 + 0.1 * FMT[picks]   # grade by rule
        adv = (rewards - rewards.mean()) / (rewards.std() + 1e-8)   # score relative to group mean
        for a, A in zip(picks, adv):
            grad = -pi.copy(); grad[a] += 1                         # gradient of log π(a)
            theta += lr * A * grad / G
    return pi, hist

_, hist = train(seed=1, record=True)
print("\n[Example 3] GRPO toy training: strategy selection probabilities, average solution length, accuracy")
print("  step   direct  1-step  +check  repeat  len  acc")
for h in hist:
    if h[0] % 50 == 0:
        print(f"  {h[0]:4d}  " + "  ".join(f"{x:5.2f}" for x in h[1:5]) + f"  {h[5]:5.0f}  {h[6]:5.1%}")
found = sum(train(sd)[0][2] > 0.5 for sd in range(1, 11))
print(f"  Retrained from scratch 10 times: reached the 'check' habit {found}/10")

08

Limits and practice

AbaBotThis works for finance too! Tasks where you can check the answer, like calculating loan interest, bond yields, or making the numbers in financial statements add up, are perfect for training with a rule-based grader.
Fruit Fly ResearcherThen what about "Should I buy this stock?" There's no rule to check the answer. That brings us back to the human preferences from Ch. 8, and the sycophancy problem comes back with them.
LimitationWhat it means
Strong where answers can be gradedRule-based rewards work best on tasks with checkable answers, like math and coding. For tasks without a single right answer, such as writing, counseling, or investment decisions, human preferences are still needed.
Thinking has a costLonger solutions take more compute time and cost more. Overthinking easy questions has also been reported.
Visible reasoning ≠ the real reasonStudies have found that a model's written reasoning may not match the process it actually used to reach its answer. When a hidden bias planted in the examples changed the answer, the reasoning was observed to leave out that reason.
Gaps in rule-based gradersIf passing the tests earns +1, code that games the tests also earns +1. The reward hacking from Ch. 4 and Ch. 8 can reappear in new forms with rule-based graders too.
Source: Turpin et al. (2023), "Language Models Don't Always Say What They Think"

Closing Part 1: from inference engines to reasoning models

In the opening of Ch. 1, the Assistant (Haru) asked, "Are old reasoning engines and today's reasoning models the same thing?" Now we can put them side by side and answer.

1970s · Ch. 1

Inference engine

Humans write IF-THEN rules, check them against facts, and chain the results into conclusions. The rules make the decisions. Outside the rules, it cannot take a single step.

2024 onward · Ch. 9

Reasoning model

The language model writes its own solutions, looks back over them, and tries multiple paths. During training, rules grade the answers. The judgment is made by the trained neural network.

This table traces how "what humans decide" shifted across nine chapters.

Series conclusion. The rule base did not disappear. In Ch. 1, rules were the machine's brain. In Ch. 9, rules became the judge that trains the machine. And in Part 2, rules move once more: they become the frame that decides what a finished model may do and when it must stop when it is working for real, namely the harness and the loop.
